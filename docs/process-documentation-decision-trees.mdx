## Context

A team I supported relied on a complex set of technical procedures and approval
steps that had never been written down end to end. Tribal knowledge filled the
gaps. Onboarding was slow, the same questions kept reaching support, and the
answer usually depended on which colleague you happened to ask.

## Audience and problem

Internal contributors needed step-by-step guidance so they could act with
confidence rather than checking first. External users needed the process to be
transparent enough to see who was responsible for which data and which decisions
at each step. And the subject-matter experts needed something they could maintain
without rewriting it from scratch every quarter, which meant the format mattered
as much as the content.

## My approach

I treated the procedure itself as the artefact to design, not just to describe.

- **Analysed the existing process** by reading specs, tickets and incident notes.
  Incident notes are the most honest documentation an organisation has: they
  record what actually happened rather than what was supposed to.
- **Interviewed subject-matter experts** to surface the decisions and edge cases
  that lived only in conversation.
- **Identified where ownership was genuinely unclear**, as opposed to merely
  undocumented. These are different problems, and writing cannot fix the first
  one. Naming them was part of the deliverable.
- **Built step-by-step procedures, decision trees and example-based
  explanations**, so a reader facing an unusual case could reason about it rather
  than search for an exact match.
- **Validated each draft with the people who would use it**, then iterated until
  it held up against real cases instead of the tidy ones.

## Outcome and impact

A previously opaque process became transparent and manageable. Users made
decisions with more confidence, support requests dropped, and the organisation
gained a clearer view of who owns which data and actions at each step. The
guidance became a shared reference used in onboarding, in audits and in
day-to-day work. The test of process documentation is whether anyone opens it
after launch, and this one is opened.

Keeping it accurate is a governance question rather than a writing one, which is
why the procedures carry the same metadata (owner, review date) as the rest of
the documentation set, and why a stale review date is flagged by the pipeline
rather than noticed by someone.

This is also the kind of content I am most reluctant to let an agent draft. The
interview *was* the work: what an agent can produce from existing material is a
description of the process as previously documented, and the whole problem here
was that no such document existed. Drafting is safe when the answer already
exists somewhere readable. That argument is on [the practice page](/practice).

## What this sample demonstrates

- Translating complex technical requirements into guidance that holds up in real
  use
- Comfort interviewing experts and structuring tacit knowledge into something
  maintainable
- Decision trees, procedures and worked examples as primary documentation forms,
  not afterthoughts
