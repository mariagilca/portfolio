Catching inconsistency before a customer does

Context​

A product shipping to several markets had a quiet consistency problem. The same feature answered to different names across in-product strings, product copy and the help centre. Whoever wrote a page resolved the ambiguity themselves, and the next person resolved it differently.

Nobody had made a mistake. There was simply no artefact whose job it was to remember the decision, and no step in the release that would have noticed.

Audience and problem​

Product managers needed confidence that documentation and UI strings would stay aligned as features shipped. Support needed to stop fielding tickets that were really naming collisions. And anyone writing needed a single place to look up what a thing was called, because the terms that get reinvented are exactly the ones where the reasoning was never written down.

My approach​

The fix was not a style guide. It was making the correct answer cheaper to find than to re-derive, and then making the release notice when something disagreed.

  • One record per term, with the preferred name, the rejected alternatives, and (the load-bearing part) why it was resolved that way. The reasoning is what stops the question being reopened every quarter.
  • A content freeze contract so downstream work starts from stable source rather than a moving target, with the freeze date owned and published instead of merely preferred.
  • Review checkpoints and an approval matrix showing who signs off on a naming change, so can we rename this is a process rather than a negotiation.
  • Automated pre-release checks that flag stale pages, orphaned terms and strings that no longer match the agreed name. This is the part that scales: the decision is human, but finding the forty places that disagree is not.

Where the automation went​

The consistency checks were the first place I moved work from people to a pipeline, and the pattern has held everywhere since:

StepOwner
Decide the preferred name and record whyHuman, with a named owner
Find every place that disagreesAutomated, on every pull request
Judge whether a flagged difference is intentionalHuman, at review
Flag pages past their review dateAutomated, before release

Automating step 2 is what made steps 1 and 3 affordable. It is also the reason the same setup absorbed AI-drafted content later without changing shape. A machine-written page is just another change that has to pass the same gate. I have written up how I extend that to agents on the practice page.

Outcome and impact​

Naming drift is now caught at review time rather than after a customer complaint, and the release has a step whose job is to notice. Writers work from real context (definitions, screenshots and examples) instead of guessing, which improves both speed and consistency for the same effort.

What this sample demonstrates​

  • Review and governance design that scales with the product roadmap rather than with headcount
  • Automated checks as a first-class part of a documentation pipeline
  • Process design that treats naming as part of the product, not an afterthought
